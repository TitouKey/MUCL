Subject: You're building the ship that will take us to Mars. We're building the crew.

Dear SpaceX team,

Somewhere off the coast of Florida, a rocket that has already flown before
steadies itself above a drone ship. The boostback burn. The landing legs. The
"go for landing" crackling through the loop.

Seeing a rocket the size of a thirty-storey building descend from the sky and
come to a standstill on its own legs — it's magic. But magic never happens
overnight. Behind every one of those landings there is work. Years of work.
Boosters that exploded on the pad, nights in mission control, decisions made
against the odds, and people who kept believing in this project — the
exploration of space — when almost nobody else did. What looks like a miracle
on a Tuesday morning is really just belief, repeated until it became physics.

Great things always start small. Every ambition, every achievement, begins
tiny — so small that no one notices it, and often so small that no one
believes in it. And most of the time, the only reason we dare at all is that
someone older, someone who has already been there, looks at us and says: you
can do it. You can. For a whole generation — for us — SpaceX is that elder.
You are the ones who said it out loud, in a language the world could not
ignore: we can go to Mars.

We are MARS UCLouvain, a student space programme from UCLouvain in Belgium.
We are that idea, started small. Every two years, we send a crew of students
to the Mars Desert Research Station in the Utah desert — one of the most
faithful Mars analogues on Earth. For two weeks they live in isolation, in a
habitat, running real experiments in health, biology, geology and technology,
supervised by UCLouvain researchers. Since 2013, dozens of research projects
have crossed the Atlantic with us. We have no pad, no booster. We have what
every mission begins with before the engines ever exist: a crew that believes,
and classrooms full of children waiting for someone to tell them they can do
it too.

That is our job in return. When Starship lifts off, a whole generation will
watch. We make sure that generation doesn't just watch — that it builds. The
students who come home from the desert are the ones who will one day work in
companies like yours, or found their own. Somewhere in our crew there may
already be a future launch director. And in every classroom we enter, we get
to be the elder someone once was for us.

Our challenge to you: put a piece of SpaceX — a mission patch, a Crew Dragon
souvenir, a flag that has been somewhere a rocket has been — on the wall of
our habitat in the Utah desert, for two weeks. We'll bring it home, signed by
the crew, and take it into classrooms across the country. A real piece of the
future, held in small hands, telling real students the same words you once
told the world: you can do it.

And if it is not too much to ask — one more thing. As part of your support,
would it be possible for our crew to visit Starbase after the mission, or to
attend a launch with your teams? We want to show that even from Europe, space
is not a distant curiosity. We are genuinely, seriously interested — not as
spectators, but as people building towards it. Coming home from the desert to
stand at the edge of a launch pad, on ground you made famous, would be the
strongest message we could carry back to our classrooms: that the dream we
tell them about is real, close, and being built right now.

You built the ship. We're building the crew. Send us your patch — we'll be the
ones saying it to the next generation, with your name on it.

[Your name] — MARS UCLouvain
