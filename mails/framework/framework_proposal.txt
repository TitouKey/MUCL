Subject: In space, you can't order a new one. That's why we chose you.

Dear Framework team,

Four hundred kilometres above us, a machine designed in the 1980s is still
running in 2026. Still working. Still improving. Still being repaired. Nobody
who built the International Space Station can ever climb up and swap a screw
— yet it has grown, changed and been upgraded for a quarter of a century.
Modules were docked, solar arrays replaced, equipment repaired by hand, in
orbit. The ISS works because it was never finished. It was designed to change.

You built a laptop on exactly that principle. No soldered futures, no sealed
cases. Every part open, labelled, replaceable with a single screwdriver that
ships in the box. Memory, storage, keyboard, screen, battery — and when the
world moves on, a new mainboard instead of a new laptop. Where other companies
sell a finished product, you sell a beginning. And you did it all with one
phrase at the centre: respect the right to repair.

That phrase is not a slogan to us. In a space environment, it is the
difference between a mission and a rescue.

We are MARS UCLouvain, a student space programme from UCLouvain in Belgium.
Every two years, we send a crew of students to the Mars Desert Research
Station in the Utah desert — one of the most faithful Mars analogues on Earth.
For two weeks they live in isolation, 8,000 kilometres from any shop, running
real experiments in health, biology, geology and technology. Since 2013,
dozens of research projects have crossed the Atlantic with us.

In that desert, we are the ISS. When a device fails, we do not order a new
one. There is no delivery. There is only us, a screwdriver, and a machine
designed to be understood. Modularity is not a comfort in our world — it is
survival. The station lasted because its design said yes when something
broke. The equipment that comes with us must say the same. Your laptops are
the only ones that do.

And there is the other word that matters: adaptability. A mission never goes
as planned. The ISS changed its orbit, its modules, its very solar arrays —
because the hardware was allowed to become something else. That is what your
Expansion Cards mean: a machine that changes with its user, port by port,
module by module, generation by generation. In space, the tool that survives
is the one that can become something new. That is what it means for hardware
to be alive.

We want the next generation to think that way too. In our classrooms, repair
is taught as a first instinct, not a last resort — because the engineers who
will one day build habitats on Mars will be the ones who learned that a
broken thing can become a whole thing again.

Our challenge to you: give our crew two Framework laptops for the mission.
Let them live in the desert — red dust in the vents, cold nights, two weeks
of isolation, real research, real data. Let our crew repair them on site, by
hand, with the screwdriver you put in the box, on camera. We'll bring them
home working, and tell every classroom: this is how you build things meant to
last. This is how the ISS was built. This is how Mars will be.

In space, nothing gets thrown away. Neither should our tools — nor our dreams.
