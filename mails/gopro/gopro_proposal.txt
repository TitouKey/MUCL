Subject: We're going to a place that looks like another planet. Bring your cameras.

Dear GoPro team,

Somewhere between the red dunes and the Utah sky, a spacesuit turns its helmet
toward the horizon. From inside that helmet — a tiny camera, recording. The
footage looks like it came from another planet. Because it's the closest thing
to it on Earth.

Every major moment of the space conquest has been filmed. The first launches,
trembling on the pad. The successes — a flag, a footprint, a voice crackling
from farther than any human voice had ever travelled. And the defeats. The
ones we would rather not watch, and that we watch anyway. Because a generation
that watches its heroes leave must also be able to look at the moments they
didn't come back from. The images were never decoration. They were memory. We
film not to show off — we film so that nothing is forgotten: not the
victories, not the costs, not the lessons that made the next launch possible.

GoPro has spent its life filming moments people want to remember. From a
surfboard off the coast of Australia to the edge of space with Red Bull
Stratos, to the cameras that quietly orbit Earth aboard the ISS. When humans
do something extraordinary, there is usually a GoPro there to prove it. And
when they try and fail, there is often a GoPro there to remember it.

We are about to do something extraordinary — and we want it recorded, not for
us, but for the ones who come after.

We are MARS UCLouvain, a student space programme from UCLouvain in Belgium.
Every two years, we send a crew of students to the Mars Desert Research
Station in the Utah desert — one of the most faithful Mars analogues on Earth.
For two weeks they live in isolation, run real experiments in health, biology,
geology and technology, and bring the mission back to classrooms across the
country. Since 2013, dozens of research projects have crossed the Atlantic
with us. The desert will punish everything we bring: red dust in every hinge,
wind on every lens. The hardware we carry has to survive — and the story we
bring back has to last.

That's where you come in.

Our challenge to you: strap GoPros to our spacesuits, mount them inside the
habitat, on the rovers, on our helmets. Let the crew film the whole mission in
first person — EVAs under the red sun, the quiet of the habitat at night,
experiments in the dust. In return, we'll hand you back the most extreme
camera test on Earth, and footage that will make people ask, out loud: "Wait,
is that really Earth?" Then we'll take that footage into classrooms across the
country — and add it to the long archive of filmed moments that tells the
human story of space. One more page in the memory of the species. One more
generation that will not forget, because someone was there, recording.

The extraordinary is closer than you think. You just have to record it.

Send us the cameras. We'll bring back the footage — and the memory. Deal?
